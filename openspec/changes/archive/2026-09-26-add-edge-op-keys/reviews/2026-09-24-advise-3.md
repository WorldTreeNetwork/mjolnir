## Independent take (10 lines)

1. Pin a stable edge XID through an explicit trusted channel; DNS and discovery may supply addresses only.
2. Require verifiable binding from that XID to the stable public key and onward to each operational public key.
3. Keep the stable private key unnecessary for ordinary sessions; an operational key must never authorize its own successor.
4. Distinguish generating a key from authorizing and activating it, including first boot with the stable signer offline.
5. Pin signed bytes, signature purpose, algorithms, key identifiers, validity bounds, and permitted operational uses.
6. Define rotation overlap and capability expiry together so planned rotation neither strands valid sessions nor grants indefinite authority.
7. Refuse compromised, revoked, expired, or superseded authority even when its signature remains mathematically valid; state the offline freshness limit.
8. Keep private keys and temporary writes outside the snapshotted volume with restrictive permissions and validated resolved paths.
9. Make persistence crash-consistent and restore explicit; refuse silent identity replacement and state how rollback is detected or bounded.
10. Preserve hosted /term and current API authentication; accept periodic offline signing work as the cost of a truly offline trust anchor.

## Comparison and steelman

The design answers much of the independent take. Decisions 1–3 preserve an explicitly pinned identity and address-only discovery. Decision 6 adequately separates startup generation from activation and permits normal operation with the stable private key offline. Decision 8 covers restrictive creation permissions, path aliases, and atomic matching bundles. Decision 9 adds permission checks at open, an inception-key binding, a named delegation domain, and capability expiry bounded by delegation expiry. The two new delta scenarios reject an attacker document carrying the victim XID and a capability exceeding its delegation lifetime. These are meaningful improvements; the original missing-binding example is now explicitly forbidden.

The strongest argument for acceptance is that “durable file outranks,” “monotonic log,” and “fails closed” express enough intent for act to fill in. I accept that file layout, concrete lifetimes, hardware custody, and transport can remain implementation choices or deferred work. Offline revocation need not be instant: the stated stale-verifier exposure until delegation expiry is an acceptable explicit tradeoff. Nor does this review require hardware rollback resistance.

However, the remaining wording permits opposite authorization decisions for the same evidence. A shorter signed expiry is not yet a defined revocation object; a prefix is precisely the stale state the recovery rule promises to refuse; and a leaked stable key can still authorize fresh operational keys after the operator rotates. Those are observable trust decisions within this node's scope, not storage mechanics or requests for a competing architecture.

## Required amendments

### R3c — Complete the supersession contract and its signed representation

Evidence: `design.md:103–111`, together with `:50–54` and `:74–83`; delta `specs/edge-auth/spec.md:84–91`.

Decision 9 names an ordered delegation array, but `supersedes` is absent from that array. It then describes supersession as a signature over the same domain “with supersedes=kid and a new exp.” It does not define how that field enters the signed bytes, how a verifier identifies a revocation rather than a grant, or how `exp=now` interacts with Decision 5's rejection of expired delegations. “Later” also has no authenticated ordering rule. Persisting a file cannot settle these interpretation differences.

**Concrete failure:** K1's original delegation expires at T. The operator issues its supersession at R, with R < T and supersession `exp=R`. A verifier first sees that record at R+1. Applying the documented positive-delegation expiry check discards the supersession, leaving K1 valid until T, despite the promise that an informed verifier rejects immediately. Another verifier could honor it indefinitely. Both readings fit the current text.

Define or precisely reference the canonical signed encoding for grants and supersessions, including field types/order, signed target and edge identity, and the distinct validation/effect rules. A new domain is optional; an unambiguous authenticated record type is sufficient. Say when a valid supersession takes effect, how it survives its own time fields and verifier restart, and how replay of an older grant is rejected. Clarify how long revocation evidence must remain, independently of positive verification material. Align “not-yet-expired superseded kids only” with graceful overlap: K1 can remain valid after K2 and K3 are issued without being revoked.

Carry the contract into the delta and add scenarios for modified supersession target, first receipt after its `exp=now`, learned supersession followed by restart/old-grant replay, and K1→K2→K3 with still-valid unrevoked K1 evidence. Retain the accepted offline exposure bound; no online revocation service is requested.

### R4c — Reject stale-prefix activation and identify the recovery authority

Evidence: `design.md:91–93` and `:113–120`. The delta has no restore or interrupted-activation scenario.

Decision 9 allows a restored delegation set that “is a prefix of” already-observed kids. That is the unsafe direction for activating the restored state. It also names authoritative state without identifying the input that remains available when the auth bundle and its colocated log have been rolled back. The new fail-closed sentence is useful, but an old bundle containing an internally consistent old log does not advertise that newer state is missing.

**Concrete failure:** backup B contains active K1 and log `[grant K1]`. Current observed state is `[grant K1, revoke K1, grant K2]`. B satisfies the stated prefix condition while reviving K1. If the old log is restored with B and nothing else is required, the host cannot distinguish B from its legitimate earlier state. Rolling back an interrupted activation must also avoid discarding an already-durably-learned revocation.

Specify the supported declared-restore procedure, which authenticated current evidence or operator reconciliation it requires, and the missing-evidence behavior. A prefix may be accepted as an input to reconciliation, but must not be activated before incorporating the authoritative revocations/current state. Require the resulting activated state to preserve all already-observed revocations; specify whether ordering is by signed log position, another authenticated relation, or a simpler explicit recovery procedure. An operator-gated restore is sufficient; transparent detection of arbitrary whole-disk rollback is not required.

Reconcile Decision 7's backup guidance: a backup cannot supply a supersession that was never signed. Waiting for expiry removes old authority but does not authorize a successor. Add delta cases for the stale prefix above, missing recovery authority, and interrupted activation after revocation was learned. Include the promised permission-at-open failure scenario while completing the persistence contract.

### R4d — Stable-private compromise cannot be repaired by operational rotation alone

Evidence: `design.md:117–119` explicitly makes re-pinning optional after the stable private key leaks. No independently authorized root recovery mechanism is defined.

**Concrete failure:** after the legitimate operator supersedes every known operational key, the attacker uses the stolen stable private key to sign a fresh delegation for a previously unseen K_attacker with a future expiry. It satisfies the same pinned-XID signature rules as the legitimate replacement. Revoking the known operational kids does not remove the attacker's root signing authority.

Require a new identity and explicitly trusted new pin after stable-private compromise, unless this contract precisely references an authenticated recovery mechanism whose authority the stolen key cannot reproduce. Preserving the pin is safe only under that additional mechanism; “optionally re-pin” alone is not a recovery rule. Keep this distinct from online operational-key compromise, which can use stable-key supersession, and root loss without leakage, which can use valid backup recovery. Add the stolen-root/fresh-delegation scenario to the delta and state how clients stop trusting the old pin.
